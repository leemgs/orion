\section*{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The two-ratio description separates two questions that are often conflated in
hierarchical-memory optimisation: whether the active data can reside in the
fast tier, and whether compulsory transfer can be hidden behind computation.
The resulting labels are operational rather than universal phases:
$\theta_B=1$ follows exactly from the overlap definition, whereas
$\theta_C=0.5$ is a declared midpoint that another application may replace
without changing Equation~\eqref{eq:lower_bound_regime}.

The processor probes show that latency depends strongly on working-set
residency but do not validate a sharp boundary at $R_C=0.5$. The accelerator
runs exercise real DMA and asynchronous CUDA streams on three devices of two
vendors: the residency direction is consistent on all three, and the T4 sweep
contains points on both sides of $\theta_B=1$. The data do not establish
per-device boundary \emph{values}, HBM- or collective-communication effects at
scale, or the generality of the three labels across model families.

The framework is most useful as a measurement discipline. Reporting $R_C$ and
$R_B$ alongside latency makes hidden assumptions about residency and overlap
inspectable, and prevents a common circularity: using total step duration both
in a bandwidth ratio and as the outcome being explained. Its relevance is not
tied to one model family. Weights, KV caches, embeddings, feature maps and
retrieval indices are different objects, but each admits the same audit:
declare the active bytes and usable fast tier, measure compulsory cross-tier
bytes and sustained bandwidth, and isolate compute time.
Equations~\eqref{eq:nonresident_fraction} and~\eqref{eq:exposed_fraction} then
state which part of the pressure cannot be removed by placement or ideal
overlap. This creates a common reporting layer under otherwise incomparable
offloading, compression, scheduling and memory-pooling studies. Whether it
predicts application outcomes is a hypothesis for production workloads, not a
result of the present probes.

The analysis supports one practical conclusion: capacity and transfer pressure
should not be inferred from latency alone. A defensible comparison should report
the minimal field set in Table~\ref{tab:contract}, which fixes the denominator
of each ratio, separates a failed directional prediction from a merely small
effect, allows threshold sensitivity to be recomputed under another residency
convention, and lets an independent reader recompute $(R_C,R_B)$ without the
original latency trace. This reporting contract---not any single measured
number---is the principal reusable output of the study.

Several limitations are material. The present evidence comes from one
processor and three accelerators (two GPUs and a TPU) with small synthetic
probes; it does not include production models, concurrent requests, energy
measurements, policy comparisons or unseen-hardware classifier tests. The
standard deviations describe repeated trials on one machine, not a population
of platforms; the accelerator ratios compare repeated windows within single
runs and quantify neither run-to-run uncertainty nor a hardware population; and
no causal inference is supported. The pointer-chain endpoints span several
hierarchy transitions, so their ratio is not a universal effect size. Finally,
Equation~\eqref{eq:latency_decomp} is an accounting identity only when its terms
are defined as non-overlapping critical-path contributions; the max-form lower
bound avoids assuming that decomposition in measured systems.

A decisive follow-up should preregister operating-point grids around
$R_C=0.5$ and $R_B=1$, collect raw event traces on at least two accelerator
architectures, compare fixed orchestration policies on production serving
stacks without changing other workload parameters, and repeat the analysis
across plausible residency conventions rather than tune a threshold on the
evaluation data. Until then, the contribution is the identifiable coordinate,
its bounds and reporting contract, and a processor-and-multi-accelerator
falsification attempt---not universal accelerator validation.
