\abstract{%
Running large neural networks now depends less on arithmetic speed than on how
efficiently data move between fast and slow memory, and inference dominates the
recurring cost and energy of deployed
models~\cite{gholami2024memorywall,ma2026inference,iea2024electricity}. Many
techniques relieve this pressure, including offloading, swapping, caching and
compression~\cite{kwon2023pagedattention,flexgen2023,huang2020swapadvisor,dao2022flashattention},
yet their reported gains are hard to compare. The operating condition of each
measurement usually goes unreported, and common bandwidth measures reuse the
very latency they are meant to explain. Here we introduce a measurement
framework that describes any operating point with two independently measurable
ratios: how much of the active data can reside in fast memory, and how long
computation takes relative to the transfers it must hide. From a conservative
bound on service time we derive two floors that no placement or scheduling
policy can remove, together with a minimal set of reported quantities that lets
any reader recompute both ratios. Small probes on a server processor and on
accelerators from two vendors show that the protocol is executable and that
latency rises in the predicted direction as residency falls. The framework gives
studies of memory-bound inference a common, auditable coordinate system;
whether it predicts the fastest orchestration policy remains an open,
preregistered test.
}
