\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{A two-ratio operational model}
\label{sec:regime}

We describe a hierarchical-memory operating point using
\begin{equation}
R_C=\frac{C_{\mathrm{fast}}}{W},\qquad
R_B=\frac{B_{\mathrm{slow}}T_{\mathrm{comp}}}{D}
   =\frac{T_{\mathrm{comp}}}{T_{\mathrm{transfer}}},
\label{eq:ratios}
\end{equation}
where $C_{\mathrm{fast}}$ is usable fast-memory capacity, $W$ is the active
working set, $D$ is compulsory cross-tier traffic per step,
$B_{\mathrm{slow}}$ is sustained bandwidth on the limiting link, and
$T_{\mathrm{transfer}}=D/B_{\mathrm{slow}}$. Unlike a ratio that uses total
step time, $R_B$ is not circular: it compares independently timed computation
and transfer intervals.

We use $\theta_C=0.50$ as an explicit majority-residency convention and derive
$\theta_B=1$ from equality of computation and transfer time. These definitions
produce three operational labels:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{capacity-limited}: $R_C<\theta_C$;
\item \textbf{I/O-limited}: $R_C\geq\theta_C$ and $R_B<\theta_B$; and
\item \textbf{coordination-dominated}: $R_C\geq\theta_C$ and
      $R_B\geq\theta_B$.
\end{itemize}
These names are screening labels, not causal bottleneck estimates: a
``capacity-limited'' point need not have capacity as its largest latency term,
and a ``coordination-dominated'' point requires residual measurements before
coordination cost can be attributed. Continuous ratios and dimensional timings
carry the evidence; the labels provide compact indexing.
The capacity boundary is a convention, not a thermodynamic critical point. The
I/O boundary is an overlap boundary: for $R_B<1$, at least
$T_{\mathrm{transfer}}-T_{\mathrm{comp}}$ cannot be hidden by ideal overlap.
Primary analyses should therefore retain continuous $(R_C,R_B)$ values. A label
near $R_C=0.5$ is convention-sensitive, whereas crossing $R_B=1$ changes the
sign of the ideal-overlap remainder. This distinction prevents a convenient
residency cut-off from being presented as an empirically discovered constant.

\subsection{Irreducible costs and falsifiable predictions}
\label{sec:model}

For accounting purposes, define non-overlapping critical-path contributions so
that a step can be written as
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{total}}=T_{\mathrm{comp}}+T_{\mathrm{mem}}+
T_{\mathrm{swap}}+T_{\mathrm{sync}}.
\label{eq:latency_decomp}
\end{equation}
Independently of that accounting decomposition, a conservative service-time
lower bound that allows the underlying services to overlap is
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{total}}\geq
\max\!\left\{T_{\mathrm{comp}},
\rho D_{\mathrm{nr}},\frac{D}{B_{\mathrm{slow}}}\right\},
\label{eq:lower_bound_regime}
\end{equation}
where $D_{\mathrm{nr}}$ is the number of non-resident bytes compulsorily
accessed in the step and $\rho$ is a lower bound on their service time per byte.
$R_C$ bounds storage residency but does not determine $D_{\mathrm{nr}}$ unless
an access-coverage model is specified. Taking the
maximum permits ideal overlap and avoids
double-counting bytes that contribute to both a cache miss and a cross-tier
transfer. Synchronisation and contention can only increase observed time, so
Equation~\eqref{eq:lower_bound_regime} is not a performance predictor. The
residual above it cannot be assigned to one latency component without
event-level measurements.

Two normalized consequences make the formulation useful beyond relabelling a
roofline plot. Under the aggregate-capacity approximation, no placement policy
can make more than $C_{\mathrm{fast}}$ bytes simultaneously resident, so the
minimum non-resident storage share of an active working set is
\begin{equation}
f_{\mathrm{nonresident}}\geq \max(0,1-R_C).
\label{eq:nonresident_fraction}
\end{equation}
Even a perfect overlap schedule leaves an exposed interval
$\max(0,T_{\mathrm{transfer}}-T_{\mathrm{comp}})$; normalized by transfer time,
\begin{equation}
f_{\mathrm{exposed}}\geq \max(0,1-R_B).
\label{eq:exposed_fraction}
\end{equation}
Thus $R_C$ answers how much placement pressure is irreducible, whereas $R_B$
answers how much compulsory-transfer service cannot be hidden. The pair also
supports controlled comparison across scales: multiplying both capacity and
working set by the same factor leaves $R_C$ unchanged, as does multiplying both
isolated service times by the same factor for $R_B$. Absolute latency still
requires the dimensional quantities in Equation~\eqref{eq:lower_bound_regime}.

This separation gives an actionable diagnostic without asserting an optimal
policy. If $R_C<0.5$, interventions can reduce the active working set, increase
usable capacity, or reduce the bytes touched while non-resident. If
$R_C\geq0.5$ but $R_B<1$, overlap alone cannot remove the exposed interval, so
reducing compulsory traffic or increasing sustained link bandwidth is
necessary. If both tests pass, the residual directs measurement toward
synchronisation, launch, contention, or locality costs. Labels describe
operating points, not devices, and can change after an intervention.

The formulation makes two falsifiable predictions. First, reducing residency
while holding the access pattern fixed should expose a higher memory-access
cost when it increases $D_{\mathrm{nr}}$. Second, when
$R_C\geq\theta_C$, configurations with $R_B<1$ must expose
transfer time even under ideal compute--transfer overlap. It does not, without
additional measurements, predict the magnitude of a strategy's speed-up or
claim that strategy rankings necessarily invert.

The two coordinates are jointly necessary but not sufficient for latency
prediction. Equal $(R_C,R_B)$ does not imply equal latency because absolute
service times, access locality, contention, and synchronisation can differ.
This non-identifiability is useful: it defines the residual phenomena that must
be measured rather than silently attributed to capacity or bandwidth.

\subsection{CPU proof-of-concept measurements}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluated working-set sensitivity on an Intel Xeon Platinum 8370C CPU
exposed through a virtualised Linux environment. The experiment used a
randomised pointer chain over working sets from 0.5 to 256~MiB and seven trials
per point. A compiled C loop performed dependent loads and timed them with
\texttt{CLOCK\_MONOTONIC\_RAW}; Python was used only to construct the chain and
summarise trials. Linux sysfs reported a 48~MiB last-level cache, which we used
as $C_{\mathrm{fast}}$. Mean dependent-load time was
$\CpuSmallMean\pm\CpuSmallSd$~ns at 0.5~MiB ($R_C=96$),
$\CpuLlcMean\pm\CpuLlcSd$~ns at 48~MiB ($R_C=1$),
$\CpuBoundaryMean\pm\CpuBoundarySd$~ns at 96~MiB ($R_C=0.5$), and
$\CpuLargeMean\pm\CpuLargeSd$~ns at 256~MiB
($R_C=0.188$), where uncertainty is one standard deviation across trials.
The non-monotonic intermediate points show that cache level, translation,
virtualisation, and system noise matter. In particular, these data do not show
a discontinuity at the conventional $R_C=0.5$ label.

As a separate harness check, we used the layered matrix program with 12 layers,
$d=512$, batch size 8, and five timing windows. It varied the fraction of layer
weights retained in the fast tier. The mean total latency averaged over the
three sampled points below $R_C=0.5$ was \MatrixLowMeanMs~ms, compared with
\MatrixFullMeanMs~ms for the fully resident point, a
\MatrixLatencyRatio-fold descriptive ratio. Because computation and copying share
the same CPU memory system, the accompanying $R_B$ sweep was noisy and is not
used as evidence for the I/O boundary.

\subsection{Accelerator proof-of-concept measurements}
\label{sec:gpu}

We ran the layered program on \NumAccel{} real accelerators of distinct
architectures and two vendors---an NVIDIA \GpuDevice{}, an NVIDIA A100, and a
Google TPU~v5e---with $d=2048$, 24 layers, batch size 8, and ten timing windows.
On the CUDA devices, computation and host-to-device transfer were timed
separately with CUDA events; non-resident layer weights were staged in pinned
host memory and copied per step, so residency ($R_C$) and overlap ($R_B$) were
varied by construction. These runs test the two falsifiable predictions of
Section~\ref{sec:model} on hardware whose device--host link, unlike a single
CPU's memory system, makes the exposed-transfer regime physically reachable.

\emph{Prediction~1 (residency) is directionally consistent on all
\NumAccel{} accelerators.} On every device, mean per-step latency in the
capacity-limited region ($R_C<\theta_C$)
exceeded that at full residency ($R_C\geq1$), by
\AccelCapRatioMin--\AccelCapRatioMax$\times$
(Table~\ref{tab:accel}). The formulation predicts this \emph{direction}, not its
magnitude; the magnitude shrinks on higher-bandwidth devices, where the offload
penalty is smaller relative to compute.

Latency is monotone in the number of offloaded (transferred) layers down to two
offloaded layers on all three devices; the only departure is the single
fully-resident point ($R_C=1$, zero transfers), which sits above the trend on
the A100 ($+9$~ms) and the TPU ($+42$~ms) but not on the slower T4. We traced
this to a measurement artifact rather than device memory behaviour: the
fully-resident point is the one operating point at which the offload code path is
skipped entirely, so it executes a structurally distinct computation---on the
TPU a separately compiled XLA graph. At $d=2048$ the true per-step memory signal
is small on the fast accelerators, so this point's one-off compilation and
launch overhead exceed the residual signal, whereas on the slower T4 the signal
dominates and monotonicity is preserved. An eager-execution control (NumPy
backend, identical sweep) shows the fully-resident point as the \emph{lowest}
latency. This control is consistent with an accelerator execution/timing
artifact but does not isolate a single cause. We have since added a discarded warm-up iteration to
the harness so that per-point compilation is excluded from future timings.

\emph{The overlap sweep spans $R_B=1$ on the T4.} Holding residency fixed and
scaling compute produced measured points on both sides of the derived boundary,
between $R_B=0.90$ and $R_B=1.19$. Their I/O-limited and
coordination-dominated labels follow from the declared rule and therefore do
not independently validate it. The run instead establishes physical
reachability of both sides with separately timed services. Testing whether the
predicted exposed interval tracks end-to-end latency requires raw event traces
and a larger sweep. On the A100 the controlled sweep stayed
compute-bound ($R_B>2$) and so did not probe the boundary, and on the TPU the
XLA path's deferred execution makes the per-op $R_B$ split unreliable; both need
a larger-workload, preregistered run. The overlap coordinate is therefore
spanned on one GPU, while the capacity direction is descriptively consistent
across three accelerators.

\subsection{Reproducibility boundary}

The repository contains the CPU raw JSONL records, per-accelerator JSON
summaries, machine-readable aggregate summaries, and export scripts used for
Tables~\ref{tab:cpu_probe} and~\ref{tab:accel}. It also contains a simulated
backend for testing the analysis pipeline. Simulated output is labelled as such
and is not used as empirical evidence. The accelerator summaries support only
the proof-of-concept comparisons reported here; they are not a substitute for
raw event traces or independent hardware replication.
